DataQuest

UX Design · Data exploration

How do you help users navigate a web of companies, products, and markets — when the same entity looks completely different depending on which data provider you ask?

My role

Sole designer. Responsible for the full design process from data model research through to shipped product. Worked directly with subject matter experts, engineering, and product.

Tools: Figma

Team: 1 designer, engineers, 1 PM

Context

DataQuest is a data traversal tool built for analysts — people who need to understand the relationships between companies, the products they offer, and the markets they operate in. It was part of a broader data platform, initially conceived as a value-add feature for subscribers.

The underlying data is genuinely complex: a single company can have dozens of subsidiaries, offer multiple products, and operate across many markets simultaneously. Different data providers model these relationships differently, and may disagree on something as fundamental as who the ultimate parent of a company is. Designing an interface for this data required understanding it first.

This was a 0→1 project in the fullest sense — no existing product to reference, no established pattern to follow, and no guarantee the concept would land with users. Everything from the navigation model to the interaction patterns to the information architecture was designed from scratch.

The problem

Analysts don't have a single starting point. Some think in terms of companies. Others start from a product and need to find the company behind it. Others start from a market and want to know what operates there. The data is the same graph; the entry point depends on the analyst's workflow.

A tool that forced a single entry point would immediately alienate a significant portion of its users. The design challenge was to make the same underlying data model feel native to all of them — without building three separate tools.

Understanding the data before designing anything

This was a greenfield project, and I came to it as a designer, not a domain expert. Before opening Figma, I spent significant time getting up to speed on the data itself — working with subject matter experts, doing independent research, and learning how the data model was structured and how different entities related to one another.

That investment was non-negotiable. You cannot design a navigation system for data you don't understand. Getting it wrong at the model level produces an interface that looks coherent but breaks the moment a real analyst tries to use it. Understanding the data shaped every structural decision that followed.

The solution: multiple entry points, one consistent shell

The design is built around a tabbed entry point system — Companies, Products, Markets — that lets users start wherever their workflow begins. Each entry point opens the same underlying detail shell, which means the interface behaves consistently regardless of how the user arrived.

Within a company detail view, four tabs give access to different facets of that entity: Details (key attributes), Products (what the company offers), Hierarchy (corporate structure), and Markets (where the company operates). The same pattern repeats across all entity types — learn it once, navigate anywhere.

Data lineage, not breadcrumbs

The navigation bar at the top of each detail view — showing "Company → Apple Inc." — looks like a breadcrumb but represents something more precise: data lineage. It shows where the entity sits in the data structure, not where the user navigated from. If you're looking at an iPhone, the lineage tells you it belongs to Apple Inc. — and that relationship is the same regardless of whether you arrived via the company list, a market, or the product directly. A conventional breadcrumb reflects the navigation path and shifts depending on how you got there. The lineage view reflects the data — consistent and always accurate.

Handling source discrepancies

One of the harder design problems was what to do when data providers model the same company structure differently — different subsidiaries, different ultimate parents, different relationship types. The temptation is to show everything at once so users can compare without switching.

I explored this. I built a side-by-side view and tested it with the team. The conclusion was clear: the volume of information on a single page worked against comparison, not for it. A proper compare feature needs dedicated design investment — layout, interaction model, conflict highlighting — not just two columns on an existing view.

The source switcher was the right call for this phase. It keeps each view clean and focused, and it turns out users found their own workaround for comparison: opening the same entity in two browser tabs with different sources selected. That wasn't designed in — it emerged from the tab model — and it's a good signal that the underlying architecture was sound.

What this project required of me

Domain knowledge as a design prerequisite

I couldn't design the navigation without understanding how the data model worked — how entities related to one another, what the hierarchy meant, where the ambiguities lived. Designers working in complex domains need to become fluent enough in the subject matter to know when a design decision is structurally wrong, not just visually awkward. Getting up to speed wasn't optional — it was the first design task.

Designing for multiple mental models simultaneously

The three entry points weren't arbitrary — they map to distinct user workflows. Getting that right required understanding not just the data model but the different jobs users were actually doing. The same information, presented from a different starting point, needs to feel native to that user's context.

Knowing when a feature is not the right solution

The side-by-side comparison view was a reasonable hypothesis. Testing it revealed it was wrong for this context — not because comparison isn't valuable, but because it needs to be a dedicated feature with proper design investment, not a layout hack on an existing view. Being able to kill your own ideas cleanly is a design skill.

Designing a system, not a screen

The consistent detail shell, the tab structure, the source switcher — none of these were designed in isolation. Each decision was made with the question: "what happens when we add the next entry point, the next entity type, the next data provider?" The system was designed to accommodate additional entry points — such as users starting from their portfolio holdings — without requiring a redesign. The answer needed to be: the same pattern, no redesign required.

Outcome

Before launch, I presented DataQuest to company leadership — walking through the design rationale, the key decisions, and how the system was architected to scale beyond its initial scope. That presentation was an important moment: it wasn't just a design review, it was making the case for why the interaction model was sound and why the product was ready. The reception was positive and the feature moved forward to launch.

DataQuest shipped as a free feature for platform subscribers. The response was stronger than anticipated — user feedback was overwhelmingly positive, with users finding genuine utility in being able to traverse company, product, and market relationships in a single tool. The signal was clear enough that the product team made a significant decision:

What launched as a free value-add for subscribers became a standalone paid feature — driven entirely by user demand rather than a planned monetisation strategy.

That transition is the clearest measure of success available: users valued it enough that the business could charge for it.

What's next

  • A dedicated compare feature — structured side-by-side views of the same entity across two data sources, designed properly rather than as a layout workaround.
  • Portfolio as an entry point — allowing users with holdings data in the platform to start from their own positions and traverse from there.
  • Conflict surfacing — proactively flagging where data providers disagree on a key attribute, rather than leaving the user to discover discrepancies by switching manually.

What I'd do differently

I'd build a proper compare feature from the start, or at least design the architecture to accommodate one. The source switcher is a good interim solution, and the browser tab workaround users found is genuinely clever — but the underlying need for structured provider comparison is real and deserved a first-class design response from day one.
